Fixing the Foundation — Repairing Broken Mailboxes Before Sending a Single Cold Email

Workhall

The Situation

I joined Workhall as a GTM Strategist to help build out our outbound prospecting engine. By the time I came on board, a lot of the strategic groundwork was already in place — we had a defined ICP, and I quickly layered on top of it: pulling and filtering prospect lists in Apollo against that ICP, and building the touchpoint sequence and cold email framing we'd use to reach them.

On paper, that meant we were close to launch. In practice, the mailboxes we were about to send from were still warming up — and when I checked their status, several were flagged as unhealthy rather than healthy in Apollo's inbox placement checks. That one detail meant everything else I'd built so far was at risk of never being seen at all.

Why "Unhealthy" Mailboxes Made Everything Else Irrelevant

It's easy to assume that once your ICP is sharp and your copy is good, the rest takes care of itself. It doesn't. An inbox placement test in Apollo doesn't just tell you whether an email was sent — it tells you where it actually landed. A mailbox marked unhealthy means a meaningful share of test sends were going to spam, to promotions, or not arriving at all, which usually points to weak sender reputation or a domain configuration issue rather than anything wrong with the message itself.

That was the moment the job changed for me. I wasn't just writing cold emails anymore — I was making sure they had somewhere to land.

Slowed the send volume down deliberately New and recently-warmed mailboxes lose trust fast if they're pushed too hard too early. I brought sending limits down to a conservative daily volume per mailbox rather than the default, giving each inbox room to build a track record before scaling back up.
Verified authentication end to end Domain authentication is invisible until it's broken. I made sure SPF, DKIM and DMARC were correctly configured and aligned for every sending domain — the kind of thing that looks fine at a glance but quietly tanks deliverability if it's even slightly off.
Re-ran inbox placement tests as a checkpoint, not a one-off Rather than treating the initial "unhealthy" result as a single verdict, I used it as a baseline and re-tested after each change, tracking the shift from mostly spam/promotions placement toward the inbox before allowing the campaign to scale.
Protected the warm-up instead of rushing it It's tempting to interrupt warm-up the moment a launch date is approaching. I held the line on giving mailboxes the runway they needed, because restarting a damaged sender reputation costs far more time than waiting a few extra days.

What Changed

Once the mailboxes cleared into healthy status, the campaign we'd been building toward — the ICP, the lists, the sequence, the copy — finally had a real chance to be read by the people it was written for. The campaign is live and running now, sending against the ICP-filtered lists through the sequence we built, from mailboxes that are actually landing in the inbox instead of quietly disappearing into spam.

None of the upstream strategy work would have mattered if I hadn't stopped to fix this first. A perfectly targeted list and a sharp cold email mean nothing if the message never arrives.

The best cold email in the world is worthless if it never leaves the spam folder. Deliverability isn't an IT afterthought — it's a GTM decision.

The key takeaway

Go-to-market strategy usually gets discussed in terms of positioning, targeting and messaging. But the infrastructure underneath — domain health, mailbox reputation, inbox placement — decides whether any of that strategy ever reaches a human being. Treat it as a first-class part of the GTM build, not a technical detail to delegate and forget.
